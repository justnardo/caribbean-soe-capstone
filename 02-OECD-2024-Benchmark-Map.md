## PART A — The TRUE structure of the 2024 Guidelines

The 2024 revision has **seven chapters**, each headed by a single lead Guideline, followed by supporting Guidelines and sub-Guidelines, then a set of "Annotations" (commentary + country practice).

| # | Chapter title (exact) | Number of sub-guidelines |
|---|---|---|
| I | Rationales for state ownership | I.A – I.D |
| II | The state's role as an owner | II.A – II.F (with II.F.1 – II.F.8) |
| III | State-owned enterprises in the marketplace | III.A – III.H (with III.C.1–2, III.F.1–4) |
| IV | Equitable treatment of shareholders and other investors | IV.A – IV.D (with IV.A.1–5) |
| V | Disclosure, transparency and accountability | V.A – V.E (with V.A.1 – V.A.10) |
| VI | The composition and responsibilities of the boards of state-owned enterprises | VI.A – VI.J |
| VII | State-owned enterprises and sustainability | VII.A – VII.C (with sub-items) |

**What changed in 2024 (useful for your literature review — shows the benchmark is live, not settled):**
- New **Chapter VII on sustainability**, which absorbed the old Chapter V "Stakeholder relations and responsible business."
- Sharper emphasis on the state as an **"active, informed and professional owner."**
- New/strengthened language on **competitive neutrality** (level playing field), **public policy objectives**, board **competencies, integrity and objectivity**, and **digital technologies** in reporting systems.
- The 2015 edition is still worth citing for comparison if you are tracking drift in the standard over a decade.

**Definitions that matter for your case-selection (verbatim, "Applicability and definitions", p. 9 of the PDF):**
> "Any undertaking recognised by national law as an enterprise, and in which the state exercises ownership or control, should be considered as an SOE. This includes joint stock companies, limited liability companies and partnerships limited by shares. Moreover, **statutory corporations, with their legal personality established through specific legislation, should be considered as SOEs if their purpose and activities, or large parts of their activities, are of an economic nature.**"

*Why this matters for you:* it gives you a citable basis for including both **statutory corporations** (e.g. Bahamas statutory bodies, Barbados statutory corporations) **and** **government-owned companies** (e.g. Nassau Flight Services Ltd) in the same study. Use this quote in Chapter 3 to justify your sample.

## PART B — The critical finding: the OECD has almost no "consequences" provision

**This is the single most important thing in this file. Read it twice.**

A full-text search of the 82-page 2024 Guidelines for *consequence(s), sanction, penalty, withholding, dismissal, remedial, enforcement action, held to account* returns **no dedicated chapter, no dedicated Guideline, and no sub-Guideline on consequences for underperformance.**

The consequence-adjacent material is **scattered across four chapters and buried in the Annotations** (i.e. the non-binding commentary, not the instrument proper):

| Location | Exact wording | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Annotations to Ch. V (integrity) | "adherence to the SOE's values is rewarded and **breaches of law are met with dissuasive consequences or penalties**" | Annotation only — and it concerns breaches of *law*, not performance failure |
| Annotations to Ch. VI, on VI.B | "**One key function of SOE boards should be the appointment and dismissal of CEOs. Without this authority it is difficult for SOE boards to fully exercise their monitoring function and assume responsibility for SOEs' performance.**" | Annotation only — but this is the closest thing to a performance consequence in the whole instrument |
| Annotations to Ch. VI, remuneration | "The introduction of **malus and claw-back provisions is considered good practice.** They grant the enterprise the right to withhold and recover remuneration from executives in cases of managerial fraud and other circumstances…" | "Good practice," non-binding |
| Annotations to Ch. VI, on VI.I (board evaluation) | "Good practice calls for the evaluation to lead to a **mandatory remedial action plan** and for performance against that plan to be reviewed annually or on a regular basis." | "Good practice," non-binding |
| Annotations to Ch. VII | "…possible **corrective actions the company intends to take to address the underperformance against a target**" | Disclosure expectation, not a consequence |
| II.F.3 (binding) | "Setting and monitoring the implementation of broad mandates and expectations for SOEs, including on **financial targets**, capital structure objectives, risk tolerance levels and sustainability…" | Binding — but sets and monitors *targets*, attaches **no consequence** for missing them |
| II.F.4 (binding) | "Setting up **reporting systems** that allow the ownership entity to regularly monitor and assess **SOE performance**…" | Binding — monitoring obligation only |
| V.A.1 + Annotation (binding + annotation) | "A clear statement to the public of **enterprise objectives and their fulfilment**…"; "SOEs should report on how they fulfilled their objectives by **disclosing key performance indicators.**" | Disclosure only |
| Annotations to Ch. II, on performance monitoring | "The ownership entity should develop the appropriate devices and select proper valuation methods to **monitor SOEs' performance** based on their established objectives. It could be helped… by developing **systematic benchmarking of SOE performance**, with private or public sector entities, both domestically and abroad. For SOEs with no comparable entity against which to [benchmark]…" | Annotation; also note the OECD already concedes the **no-comparator problem** (your constraint **C4**) |

### How to use this — three moves that will earn you marks

1. **Do not treat this as a flaw you have to hide. Treat it as a finding.** Your thesis is "consequences without owners." The benchmark itself is *constructed around ownership, targets, monitoring and disclosure — and is thin on consequence*. So a Caribbean state that transposes the OECD text faithfully will still import a **consequence gap**. That is a genuinely original and defensible contribution, and it reframes P2: the Consequence Gap is not only an implementation failure in small states, it is **partly designed in**.

2. **Because D5 has no single OECD home, you must build it by aggregation — and say so.** Your D5 "Consequences" dimension should be assembled from **II.F.3 + VI.B + VI.I + V (integrity) + VII (corrective action)**. Document this construction in Chapter 3. A marker who checks your D5 against the OECD text will find no "Chapter on Consequences" — pre-empt that by explaining the construction explicitly. This turns a vulnerability into evidence of rigour.

3. **Separate your two levels of claim.** Distinguish:
   - *de jure low D5* = the law is silent or aspirational (a **drafting** gap), from
   - *de facto low D5* = the law has teeth but they are never used (an **enforcement** gap).
   Then apply **Gapk = Dk(de jure) − Dk(de facto)** as the manual instructs. Your most interesting cells are where de jure is *already* low (drafting gap) **and** de facto is low (enforcement gap) — that is "consequences without owners" in its purest form.

## PART C — The OECD's own escape hatch for your SIDS argument

**Quote this early in Chapter 2 and again in Chapter 5.** "Applicability and definitions" states:

> "The Guidelines are generally applicable to SOEs, whether they operate domestically or internationally. The Guidelines recognise that **no one size fits all** and different legal and administrative traditions may call for different arrangements. The Guidelines are therefore **outcomes-based**, meaning that it is the role of governments to decide how to achieve the outcomes that they recommend."

And note the **absence**: a full-text search for *small state, small island, SIDS, microstate, developing country, capacity constraints* returns **no substantive treatment** of small states anywhere in the 2024 Guidelines. Neither does the instrument offer a hardship or phased-compliance route.

**Two-sentence argument you can now make with citable support:**
> The OECD concedes that "no one size fits all" and that the Guidelines are outcomes-based, yet the instrument provides no guidance on how a state with a small talent pool, no domestic comparators, and recurring exogenous shocks is to achieve those outcomes. This asymmetry is precisely why transfer to Caribbean SIDS must be *adapted rather than copied* — and why the recommendations in Chapter 5 must be feasibility-tested against constraints C1–C6.

## PART D — PROPOSED: The PCGI 21-indicator architecture (7 dimensions × 3 indicators)

**Status: a proposal for you to adopt, adapt, or replace.** The manual never publishes its list, so you own this design decision. Whatever you choose, lock it before you start scoring and justify it in Chapter 3 (say why these 7 and not others, and why 3 each).

**Design logic:** the OECD chapters are organised by *governance domain*. Your research question is about the **performance-consequence chain**. So this architecture follows the chain — **Own → Target → Monitor → Disclose → Consequence → Board → Audit** — and grounds each indicator in specific citable OECD sub-guidelines. This is what makes P2 testable: D5 sits at the end of a chain, and a chain is only as strong as its weakest link.

> Every indicator below cites the exact OECD provision to score against. Copy these citations straight into your Excel sheet — that satisfies the manual's "no quote means no score" rule from the outset.

### D1 — Ownership & mandate *(OECD Ch. I; II.A–II.E)*
| ID | Indicator | OECD basis |
|---|---|---|
| D1.1 | Clear, published ownership policy stating the state's objectives as an owner | I.A, I.C, I.D |
| D1.2 | Ownership function centralised in a single designated ownership entity with a defined mandate | II.D |
| D1.3 | Ownership entity has defined capacity/competencies and is itself accountable to a representative body | II.E, II.C |

### D2 — Objectives & targets *(presumes someone sets the targets)*
| ID | Indicator | OECD basis |
|---|---|---|
| D2.1 | Individual SOEs have financial/operational targets and broad mandates set for them | II.F.3 |
| D2.2 | Non-commercial / public service obligations separately identified, costed and disclosed | III.C, III.C.1, III.C.2 |
| D2.3 | Targets and objectives disclosed publicly (not internal-only) | V.A.1; Annotations to Ch. V |

### D3 — Monitoring & reporting systems
| ID | Indicator | OECD basis |
|---|---|---|
| D3.1 | A formal reporting system exists enabling the owner to monitor and assess SOE performance regularly | II.F.4 |
| D3.2 | Regular (at least annual) aggregate reporting on the SOE portfolio as a whole | V.E |
| D3.3 | The monitoring body holds adequate accounting/audit competence and uses benchmarking or digital tools | II.F.4; Annotations to Ch. II (esp. on benchmarking) |

### D4 — Transparency & disclosure
| ID | Indicator | OECD basis |
|---|---|---|
| D4.1 | SOEs publish financial and operating results of the enterprise | V.A.2 |
| D4.2 | Financial assistance from the state — subsidies, guarantees, budget loans — is disclosed | V.A.7, V.A.8 |
| D4.3 | SOEs report on fulfilment of objectives via key performance indicators | V.A.1, V.A.2; Annotations to Ch. V |

### D5 — Consequences & enforcement ⭐ *the dimension your thesis predicts is lowest*
| ID | Indicator | OECD basis |
|---|---|---|
| D5.1 | Explicit consequence or sanction attached to non-achievement of targets, or to failure to report | Aggregated: II.F.3 + Ch. V integrity annotation (no single OECD home — document this) |
| D5.2 | The board (or owner) has the power to appoint **and remove** the CEO, with managerial consequences actually available | VI.B; Annotations to Ch. VI, on VI.B |
| D5.3 | A corrective/remedial mechanism follows evaluation, audit failure or underperformance against a target | VI.I + Annotations; VII (corrective action); malus/clawback as "good practice" |

### D6 — Board composition & accountability
| ID | Indicator | OECD basis |
|---|---|---|
| D6.1 | Merit-based, transparent and structured board nomination process | II.F.2 |
| D6.2 | Independence requirements; safeguards against political interference and conflicts of interest (incl. cooling-off) | VI.C, VI.D, VI.E |
| D6.3 | Board held accountable: board evaluation + remuneration policy aligned to long-term enterprise interest | II.F.8, VI.B, VI.I |

### D7 — Audit & independent oversight
| ID | Indicator | OECD basis |
|---|---|---|
| D7.1 | Internal audit function with the capacity and autonomy to operate, reporting to the board/audit committee | V.C |
| D7.2 | Annual external audit by an independent, competent auditor to internationally recognised standards | V.D |
| D7.3 | Independent state control organs and a formal link to legislative/representative oversight | II.F.6; Ch. V (accountability); II.E |

**Totals: 7 dimensions × 3 indicators = 21 indicators. Max score 3 each = 63.**
Your manual's formula is therefore `PCGI = (Σ si / 63) × 100`, and `Dk = mean of the 3 indicator scores for dimension k` — both consistent with the above. ✔

### Two things to guard against
- **"3 indicators per dimension" is a construction, not a natural fact.** A marker may ask why 3 and not 4 or 5. Say plainly: equal weighting across dimensions was chosen for transparent comparability across four countries, and the 63-point ceiling is interpretable as a percentage. Cite the composite-index guidance (OECD/JRC Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators) in your methods section for the weighting choice.
- **Do not let the OECD chapters do double duty.** If you later find that D1 and D6 are pulling on the same underlying OECD provisions, you can still justify it — an ownership policy and a board's independence are separate *mechanisms* even where the OECD text overlaps. Just be explicit.
