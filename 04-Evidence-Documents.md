## 2. Public Accounts Committee (PAC) reports

**Why PAC reports matter more than you might expect:** they are the point at which a finding becomes a **question**, and the answer becomes a **record**. Where an AG report shows a failure and the PAC record shows that nothing followed, you have documented the **consequence gap in its purest form** — and you can quote it.

| Country | Where to look | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Trinidad and Tobago** ⭐⭐ | **`https://www.ttparliament.org/committees/show/paec/`** ✅ 200 — the **Public Accounts (Enterprises) Committee (PAEC)** | **Structurally distinctive — see below** |
| Jamaica | `https://japarliament.gov.jm/` ✅ — Hansard and committee records. ⚠️ Jamaica's committee reports are branded the **Public Administration and Appropriations Committee (PAAC)**, not "PAC". Search **both** terms | Locate |
| Barbados | `https://www.barbadosparliament.com/` ✅ — PAC reports. **No Barbados PAC reports located online** — assume a genuine gap and record it | ⚠️ Gap |
| The Bahamas | Parliament of The Bahamas — PAC reports are **not published online**. Contact the House of Assembly: **`hoa@bahamas.gov.bs`** · +1 242 322 2041 / 2042 | ⚠️ Gap |

**⭐ Trinidad and Tobago's PAEC is the single most interesting institutional finding in this section.** T&T **uniquely splits parliamentary audit oversight in two**: an ordinary PAC for ministries and departments, and a **separate Public Accounts (Enterprises) Committee dedicated solely to State Enterprises**, with its standing mandate drawn from the Constitution (s.119). Of your four cases, it is the only one whose legislature has built a specialist SOE-oversight body at all.

Use it three ways:
1. **D7.3 and D5.1 de jure scoring** — an SOE-only committee is a formally stronger oversight consequence than a general PAC with an overcrowded docket. That is a citable institutional difference between your cases.
2. **The de facto test that follows** — a committee that exists but whose reports are thin, or whose recommendations go unanswered, is *the consequence gap in its purest form*. Score the interval, not the mandate.
3. **A transferable recommendation** — if T&T's PAEC produces consequence and the others' general PACs do not, you have an **empirically grounded, regionally realistic recommendation** rather than one borrowed from the OECD. That is exactly what your manual means by a contribution.

> **Do not overclaim.** A specialist committee is *structural capacity*, not proof of consequence. Check whether its reports are tabled, published, and acted on before scoring D5.1 above 2.

**What to extract:** the date the PAC/PAEC considered the AG's finding, the date of the report, and the date of any government response. **The intervals are the data.** A finding tabled in one year and considered by the PAC four years later is a de facto **0 or 1** on D7.3/D5.1 — and the arithmetic is unarguable.

> **If PAC records are thin or unavailable, say so and record it.** "No PAC report located for the period despite the PAC being constituted" is a **finding about oversight**, not a failure of your research. Log it as `NOT OBTAINED` with the reason and disclose it as a limitation.

## 3. IMF Article IV Staff Reports

Contemporaneous, comparative, and unusually willing to say in plain English that a monitoring framework "is not effectively enforced." This is often the most quotable de facto evidence you will find.

| | |
|---|---|

| **Search route** | IMF eLibrary, or a web search for *"Article IV" {country} staff report* and use the IMF-hosted PDF |
| **Frequency** | Roughly annual for all four; the Bahamas, Jamaica and Barbados have all had recent programmes or surveillance cycles worth reading |

**The specific reports to start from** — go straight to these rather than searching blind:

| Country | Report | Note |
|---|---|---|

| **Jamaica** | **Country Report No. 24/069** (March 2024) | ⚠️ **No. 24/277** (July 2024) is a **programme review**, not an Article IV — do not cite it as one |
| **Barbados** | **Country Report No. 23/436** (December 2023) | ⚠️ **No. 24/368** (December 2024) is a **programme review** — same caution. Barbados is under an EFF/RSF arrangement, so programme documents *are* useful for SOE conditionality, but label them correctly |
| **Trinidad and Tobago** | **Country Report No. 24/150** (June 2024) | Most recent full Article IV |

> ⚠️ **Access note.** IMF `imf.org/-/media/...ashx` download URLs return **403 to automated requests from this environment, even with a browser user-agent**. The reports are *not* unavailable — open them in a real browser, or use the IMF eLibrary. Record them as obtainable; this is a tooling limit, not a source gap.

**What to extract for each country:**
- Statements on **SOE performance, monitoring, and fiscal risk from SOEs** — search the text for *state-owned*, *public bodies*, *statutory*, *SOE*, *recapitalis*, *arrears*
- **Recapitalisations and guarantees** — these are your **C6 soft-budget evidence**. If a government recapitalised an SOE without conditions, that is D5.1 = 0 or 1, evidenced
- Any explicit statement about **enforcement, compliance, or the absence of consequences**
- **Fiscal risk statements** in the budget documents — the IMF frequently quantifies SOE risk, which gives you numbers

> **Useful framing for Chapter 2:** the IMF's recurring call for "enhanced SOE monitoring" across the Caribbean is itself evidence that the gap is recognised by international surveillance but persists. **Recognition without consequence is precisely your thesis** — and having an authoritative external body document it every year strengthens P2 considerably.

## 5. The Disclosure Audit (manual Week 2)

Your manual requires a **Disclosure Audit of the top 20 SOE websites**. This is your fastest source of high-quality de facto evidence, and it is entirely within your control.

### Method

1. **Assemble the sample.** Identify the principal SOEs from each country's own list. **Three of the four publish one — use them as your sampling frame, and cite the list you used.** A named, citable frame is far more defensible than "the first 20 I found":

| Country | Sampling frame | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Jamaica** | `mof.gov.jm/wp-content/uploads/Copy-of-List-of-active-PBs-at-July-31-2023-2.pdf` — the Ministry of Finance's list of **active Public Bodies** (~149 entities) | ✅ 200 — the most complete frame of the four |
| **Trinidad and Tobago** | `finance.gov.tt/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/MOF-Company-and-Statutory-Board-Listing.pdf` — Ministry of Finance **Company and Statutory Board Listing** (~52 entities) | ✅ 200 |
| **Barbados** | `https://www.gov.bb/State-Bodies` — the **State Bodies** directory | ✅ 200 but **JavaScript-rendered** — open in a browser and save the page |
| **The Bahamas** | `bahamas.gov.bs/government-corporations` | ⚠️ **403** — retrieve via a browser, or use the schedules in the PFM Act / the AG reports instead (see `03-Framework-Documents.md`, BHS-FW-01) |

**Then:** take the largest or most significant until you have ~20 across the four countries, and **write down the rule you used**.
2. **For each SOE, check for five items on the website:**

| # | Check | PCGI link |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is the **latest annual report** published? | D4.1 |
| 2 | What is the **most recent year** available, versus the current year? → **the lag in years is the datum** | D4.1, D3.2 |
| 3 | Are **audited financial statements** published, and are they qualified? | D7.2, D4.1 |
| 4 | Is there any statement of **objectives / KPIs and their achievement**? | D2.3, D4.3 |
| 5 | Is **board composition** published, with appointments and dates? | D6.1 |

3. **Score each item Yes / No / Partial, and record the date you checked.** Websites change — the check date is part of the evidence.
4. **The lag distribution is your headline.** If the median SOE publishes its annual report 2–3 years in arrears, that single statistic does more work for your argument than several pages of legal analysis.

### Output table

| SOE | Country | Annual report latest year | Lag (yrs) | Audited FS? | Qualified? | Objectives/KPIs? | Board published? | Checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

> **This is the highest-yield task in the whole project.** It is fast, fully verifiable, reproducible, uses no privileged access, and produces a hard number that directly evidences D4, D3 and — because nothing follows from non-disclosure — **D5**. Do it early.

## 6. Honest gaps to expect (record them, do not paper over them)

| Gap | Likely reality | How to handle |
|---|---|---|
| **AG reports more than ~5 years old** | Patchy; often offline or physical only | Request from the Audit Office. The Bahamas' 2006–2021 run covers you; expect less elsewhere |
| **Individual SOE audit qualifications** | Usually reported *inside* the AG's annual report, not separately | Read the AG report's SOE/statutory body sections specifically — do not assume a separate document exists |
| **T&T Auditor General site** | Not found at a standard URL | Route through Parliament |
| **PAC reports for all four** | Frequently the weakest online coverage of anything in this project | Report the absence as a finding |
| **SOE annual reports** | Highly variable; some SOEs publish nothing | **This variability is the finding.** It maps directly onto D4.1 |
| **Pre-2015 IMF Article IVs** | Available but less focused on SOE governance | Focus on the last ~5 years, as the manual specifies |

> **The rule that protects your credibility:** every unobtained document goes into the register as `NOT OBTAINED` with the reason, and into Chapter 3 as a disclosed limitation. **A marker will reward a documented gap and penalise a silent one.** Silently reducing a country's evidence base and then scoring it on thin evidence is the mistake to avoid.
