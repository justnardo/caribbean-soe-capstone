# 05 — Method, Rubric & the PCGI

This is your Chapter 3 spine, plus the operational rules that keep 84 scores defensible.

---

## 1. Method statements you can lift and adapt

> **Design.** This study adopts a comparative multiple-case study design (Yin, 2018; Eisenhardt, 1989) treating each national SOE governance framework as a case. Four Commonwealth Caribbean SIDS — The Bahamas, Jamaica, Barbados and Trinidad and Tobago — are examined against the OECD Guidelines on Corporate Governance of State-Owned Enterprises (2024).

> **Data.** The study relies exclusively on publicly available secondary data. Two corpora are maintained separately: a **framework corpus** recording what the rules require, and an **evidence corpus** recording what occurs in practice (see §4). Documents were identified through the OECD, national audit offices, ministries of finance, parliament websites and the IMF, and recorded in a Document Register with a direct URL, retrieval date and content hash for every item.

> **Instrument.** A 21-indicator Performance-Consequence Governance Index (PCGI) was constructed from the OECD Guidelines (2024), organised into seven dimensions of three indicators each: ownership and mandate; objectives and targets; monitoring and reporting; transparency and disclosure; **consequences and enforcement**; board composition and accountability; and audit and independent oversight. Equal weighting across dimensions was adopted for cross-country comparability, following the OECD/JRC Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators (2008).

> **Analysis.** De jure scoring applied directed content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005), coding statutory and regulatory text against the PCGI rubric on a 0–3 scale. Every score is supported by a verbatim quotation and section reference; no score was assigned without one. De facto scoring applied the same instrument to the evidence corpus. The implementation gap is reported as Gapk = Dk(de jure) − Dk(de facto). Qualitative material was analysed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006, 2021), with initial codes clustered into themes and each theme mapped to the SIDS constraints C1–C6.

## 2. The De Jure rubric (0–3) — what the law says

| Score | Label | Test to apply | Typical wording in the statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | **Absent** | No provision anywhere in the framework | *Silence.* Nothing to quote — record "no provision located" plus which documents were searched |
| **1** | **Aspirational** | Mentioned as good practice, in a preamble, policy statement, or non-binding guidance; a "should" | *"should", "is encouraged to", "it is desirable that", "in accordance with good practice"* |
| **2** | **Mandated** | A binding obligation — but **no** specified enforcement mechanism, sanction, or independent oversight tied to it | *"shall", "must", "is required to"* — with nothing following |
| **3** | **Mandated & Enforced** | Binding obligation **plus** a specified enforcement mechanism, sanction, or independent oversight body | *"shall … and failure to comply constitutes an offence / the Minister shall table / the Auditor General may disallow / a penalty of $X applies"* |

### The 2-vs-3 decision is where your dissertation is won or lost

This is the only genuinely hard judgement in the rubric, and **it is the judgement your P2 depends on.** If you score too generously at 3, your Consequence Gap evaporates and P2 is unsupported. If you score too harshly, everything clusters at 1–2 and the index loses discriminating power.

**Apply this test mechanically, every time:**

> Is there a **named actor** who must do something, a **defined trigger** that starts the clock, and a **specified outcome** if they do not?

All three → **3**. Any one missing → **2**.

| Wording | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "The corporation shall submit an annual report to the Minister." | **2** | Binding, but no trigger, no consequence, no named oversight of *failure* |
| "The corporation shall submit an annual report to the Minister within 90 days of year end. Where a corporation fails to do so, the Minister shall lay the fact of the default before Parliament." | **3** | Named actor, defined trigger, specified outcome |
| "Corporations should seek to achieve the performance targets agreed with the Ministry." | **1** | "Should" — aspirational. *Note: even a performance-target provision can be a 1 if phrased non-bindingly* |
| "The Board shall appoint the Chief Executive Officer." | **2** | Binding power, but no removal power, no ground, no process |
| "The Board shall appoint and may remove a Chief Executive Officer for cause, following a performance evaluation conducted under section X." | **3** | Power + trigger + process |

**Record the negative finding too.** If a score is 0, write *which* documents you searched and found silent — e.g. "No performance-consequence provision located in PFM Act 2023 Parts I–XII, nor in the accompanying Regulations." A documented absence is evidence. An undocumented absence is a guess.

## 3. The De Facto rubric (0–3) — what actually happens

Note that these anchors are **entirely different** from the de jure ones. Do not reuse the de jure anchors.

| Score | Label | Test to apply against the evidence corpus |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | **Requirement routinely not met** | Systematic, documented, repeated failure — e.g. a multi-year audit backlog; annual reports years in arrears; a monitoring unit that has never published |
| **1** | **Met only occasionally** | Inconsistent compliance; some SOEs or some years comply, others do not, with no pattern of correction |
| **2** | **Met by most SOEs, but with no consequences for non-compliance** | The *form* is observed, but compliance is the whole of the obligation — nothing follows non-compliance. **This is the modal score your thesis predicts** |
| **3** | **Met, and non-compliance triggers action** | Compliance is enforced in practice: a named official acts, a sanction lands, a board or CEO is replaced, funding is withheld, a default is reported to Parliament |

> **Score 2 is the quiet finding.** A country can look well governed on paper *and* in practice — reports filed on time, audits completed — and still be a 2, because **nothing happens to those who fail.** Expect most Caribbean cells to land here, and make that the centre of Chapter 5: *the accountability machinery runs; the consequence machinery does not.*

### Where de facto evidence actually comes from
Every de facto score must cite an evidence document, not an inference. Practical sources, in descending order of strength:

1. **Auditor General's annual report** — audit backlogs, qualified opinions, instances of unapproved expenditure, failures to comply with reporting deadlines. Hardest evidence available.
2. **PAC reports and Parliament hansard** — what was asked, and whether anything followed. Usefully blunt.
3. **IMF Article IV staff reports** — contemporaneous, comparative, and willing to say "the monitoring framework is not effectively enforced" in plain English.
4. **Budget documents / Fiscal Strategy Reports** — capital transfers, recapitalisations, and guarantees. These *are* the soft-budget evidence for C6.
5. **SOE annual reports and websites** — see the Disclosure Audit in `04-Evidence-Documents.md` §5.

**Do not use news reports as the basis of a de facto score.** Use them only to *locate* an official document, then cite the official document. A marker will accept an AG report; they may not accept a newspaper.

## 4. Framework corpus vs Evidence corpus — the rule that protects your argument

| | Framework corpus | Evidence corpus |
|---|---|---|
| **Answers** | What do the rules *require*? | What actually *happens*? |
| **Documents** | OECD Guidelines 2024; PFM Acts; Public Bodies Management & Accountability Act; State Enterprises Performance Monitoring Manual | AG reports; PAC reports; IMF Article IVs; budget annexes; SOE annual reports |
| **Feeds** | De jure scores | De facto scores |
| **Appendix** | Appendix A | Appendix B |

**Three rules:**
1. **Never** take a de facto score from a framework document. A statute that *says* reports must be filed is not evidence that they *are* filed.
2. **Never** take a de jure score from an evidence document. A news story about a bailout does not establish what the law requires.
3. **Where a document is both** — e.g. a monitoring manual that also reports compliance — record it in both appendices and be explicit about which paragraph you used for which purpose.

## 5. Computation — exactly what your manual specifies

Let `sᵢ ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}` be the score for indicator *i*.

**Dimension score** for dimension *k* (three indicators):
```
Dk = (s₁ + s₂ + s₃) / 3          (range 0–3)
```

**Total PCGI (de jure):**
```
PCGI = ( Σ sᵢ / 63 ) × 100       where 63 = 21 × 3
```
*(range 0–100)*

**Implementation gap:**
```
Gapk = Dk(de jure) − Dk(de facto)   (range −3 to +3)
```

### How to read the results — and how to avoid over-claiming

| Pattern | Interpretation | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| Dk(de jure) **high**, Dk(de facto) **low** → large positive Gapk | The classic **implementation gap** (P1). Rules exist; practice lags | Chapter 4, supporting P1 |
| Dk(de jure) **low**, Dk(de facto) **low** → small Gapk | A **drafting gap**. There is no rule to breach, so there is no implementation failure — the Commonwealth Caribbean simply never legislated for it. **This is the strongest form of your argument** | Chapter 5 |
| Dk(de jure) **high**, Dk(de facto) **high** → small Gapk on both | Genuine strength. Report it honestly — a purely negative study is less credible and less useful | Chapter 4 — do not bury these |
| Dk(de jure) **low**, Dk(de facto) **high** | Rare. Either a non-statutory practice is stronger than the law (interesting — look for it inside standard-style internal operational controls), or a scoring error. **Re-examine before reporting** | Re-check first |

**On averaging:** dimension scores are means of three ordinals. This treats the 0–3 differences as equal intervals, which is a known simplification. State it as a limitation in Chapter 3 and note that the dimensions are ordinal-comparable rather than precisely cardinal. One sentence is enough — but a marker will look for it.

## 6. Reflexive thematic analysis — how to actually run it

**Step 1 — Initial coding.** Read the evidence corpus with a highlighter. Label every relevant sentence or paragraph with a short, concrete, verbatim-adjacent code. Keep them ugly and specific:

`bailout without conditions` · `reporting arrears` · `board appointed on political cycle` · `audit qualification repeated across years` · `no removal power` · `targets set but never published` · `CEO vacancy filled without process` · `monitoring unit lacks staff` · `hurricane cited as reason for variance`

**Do not tidy codes at this stage.** Volume and specificity now buys you themes later. Log every one in `data/Reflexive_Coding_Log.csv`.

**Step 2 — Generating themes.** Cluster codes into candidate themes. Your manual's model theme is the standard to aim for:

> *"Accountability is legislated but not owned: monitoring duties are assigned to units that lack authority."*

It is good because it makes a **claim**, it names a **mechanism**, and it is **falsifiable**. Compare a weak theme — *"lack of resources"* — which claims nothing and cannot be tested.

| Weak theme | Strong theme |
|---|---|
| "Weak enforcement" | "The reporting duty sits with a unit that has neither budget nor removal power, so compliance depends on goodwill" |
| "Political interference" | "Board terms are aligned to the electoral cycle, producing a decision-making quiet period before elections" |
| "Capacity problems" | "The same four individuals sit across the audit committee, the board and the ownership unit, so independence is structurally unavailable" |

**Step 3 — Map every theme to C1–C6.** A theme that maps to no constraint is either descriptive (move it to Chapter 4) or incomplete (dig further). The mapping is what turns your themes into the Chapter 5 explanation of *why the gap is structural* — the core of the argument.

**Step 4 — Positionality.** You are an insider: your artifact is from a Bahamian SOE. Your manual requires reflexive practice, so state it plainly in Chapter 3 — *"The researcher is employed within a Bahamian state-owned enterprise and has professional familiarity with the operational reporting practices discussed. This informed the selection of practices for the Transferability Matrix but was not used as the basis of any de facto score."* That single sentence is protective. Being *reflexively explicit* is safe; being *silently* insider is the risk.
