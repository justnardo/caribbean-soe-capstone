## 1. The matrix logic (from your manual)

Score each practice **1–5** on two axes:

- **Axis 1 — Governance Fit (Expected Impact):** how directly does this close the consequence gap identified by the PCGI? Weight toward **D5**.
- **Axis 2 — SIDS Feasibility:** can a small, fiscally constrained, socially close state actually operate it? This is where **C1–C6** do their work.

| | High Feasibility (4–5) | Low Feasibility (1–3) |
|---|---|---|
| **High Fit (4–5)** | 🟢 **TRANSFER NOW** | 🟠 **ADAPT FIRST** |
| **Low Fit (1–3)** | 🟡 Transfer if capacity allows | 🔴 Retain as context |

**Your defensible claim:** the practices that survive are those that **manufacture accountability procedurally** rather than relying on an independent actor, a competitive market, or the credible threat of failure. Because C1 removes independent actors, C4 removes comparators, and C6 removes the threat of failure — the only transferable mechanisms left are the *procedural* ones.

That is a real thesis, and it is falsifiable. If a practice that depends on independence or market pressure scores well in your analysis, the argument needs revision. Good.

## 2. The shortlist (select 6–8 from these 10)

Full scoring, justifications and adaptation notes: `data/Transferability_Matrix.csv`.

### 🟢 TRANSFER NOW

**P-01 — Public quarterly SOE performance dashboard with standardised KPIs** · *Fit 5 · Feasibility 4*
*Origin:* listed-company quarterly reporting and investor KPI disclosure. Fixed reporting calendar, standard metric set; visibility alone disciplines.
*Constrained by:* C1, C3 — but only mildly.
*Why it transfers:* converts the OECD's monitoring obligation into a **public** one. Costs almost nothing because you publish what SOEs already report internally. **The cheapest D3/D4 win available.**
*Adapt:* define a **minimum common KPI set (8–12 metrics)** before publishing anything, and stagger by portfolio size.

**P-02 — Board performance evaluation leading to a mandatory remedial action plan** · *Fit 5 · Feasibility 4*
*Origin:* corporate board effectiveness reviews.
*Why it transfers:* it is **verbatim OECD "good practice"** in the Annotations to VI.I. You are not inventing a recommendation — you are noting the benchmark recommends it and the region has not adopted it. Very strong for Chapter 5.
*Adapt:* use a **CARICOM/UWI evaluator pool** rather than a local one. This weakens C1 *and* C2 in a single design choice — a nice point to make explicitly.

**P-03 — Balanced Scorecard cascade from the owner's mandate to unit level** · *Fit 4 · Feasibility 4*
*Origin:* strategy execution / performance management (Kaplan & Norton).
*Why it transfers:* converts a vague SOE mandate into measurable objectives, and forces the commercial/public-policy distinction the OECD requires at III.C.
*Adapt:* because **C4 removes true comparators**, use **self-referential** measures — year-on-year, cost per unit, service reliability — not cross-firm league tables.

**P-04 — Integrated reporting: an "objectives and their fulfilment" table in the existing annual report** · *Fit 4 · Feasibility 4*
*Origin:* integrated reporting / annual-report best practice.
*Why it transfers:* a low-cost way to satisfy OECD V.A.1 and the Ch. VII expectation of disclosing corrective action.
*Adapt:* **do not adopt a full integrated-reporting framework.** Add one table to the report that already exists. The lightweight version is the whole point.

**P-09 — Named-accountability operational reporting with mandatory exception capture and block-on-send** · *Fit 5 · Feasibility 5* ⭐
*Origin:* front-line operational control in aviation ground handling.
*Why it transfers:* **this one is already running inside a Bahamian SOE** — see `08-Insider-Practice-Note-WITHHELD.md`. Every exception compels a written reason, a named owner and the action taken, and the report **cannot be sent** until mandatory fields are complete.
*Constrained by:* C1 and C2 — and it beats both, because escalation targets are defined by role **in advance**, removing the discretionary moment where social proximity would suppress reporting.
*This is your strongest single item* — it is the only practice on the list with **implementation evidence** rather than a literature rationale.

### 🟠 ADAPT FIRST

**P-05 — Remedial / special-measures regime: a published recovery plan with a named accountable executive** · *Fit 5 · Feasibility 2*
*Origin:* corporate restructuring, turnaround and administration.
*Why Fit is 5:* it is the strongest available **substitute** for the two disciplines the OECD itself says SOEs are insulated from — **takeover and bankruptcy**. You can quote the OECD conceding this, which is powerful.
*Why Feasibility is 2:* **C6 kills it.** Government cannot credibly threaten to let an essential service fail.
*Adapt:* replace the terminal sanction (closure) with a **graduated ladder** — board replacement → CEO removal → external management contract → divestment. Each rung is survivable; the ladder as a whole is not.

**P-06 — Hard budget constraint with performance-contract tranching** · *Fit 5 · Feasibility 2*
*Origin:* contractual performance management + earned-autonomy regimes.
*Why Fit is 5:* it attacks the **soft-budget constraint** directly — the mechanism your manual names in C6.
*Why Feasibility is 2:* the region has repeatedly recapitalised essential SOEs without conditions. A statutory no-bailout rule is easy to write and near-impossible to honour in a crisis.
*Adapt:* **tranche only the discretionary investment envelope.** Accept that the standing wage bill will always be met, and make each tranche review **public**. Honest and workable beats elegant and ignored.

**P-07 — Independent audit committee with a filing deadline and automatic parliamentary escalation** · *Fit 5 · Feasibility 3*
*Origin:* audit-committee best practice and statutory filing deadlines.
*Why Fit is 5:* creates a **consequence for non-disclosure** — filling the precise gap the OECD leaves, since V.D mandates external audit but attaches no timeliness sanction.
*Adapt:* attach the escalation to an **existing** body (the PAC or a standing finance committee) rather than creating a new oversight unit. **C3 is exactly why** — a small state cannot keep funding duplicate scrutiny bodies. And heed the PAC-backlog literature: the risk is creating one more report nobody reads.

**P-08 — Executive performance-related pay with malus and clawback** · *Fit 4 · Feasibility 2*
*Origin:* listed-company remuneration design.
*Why Fit is 4:* **explicitly named as "good practice"** in the OECD Annotations to Ch. VI.
*Why Feasibility is 2:* SOE pay is politically visible and compressed, so raising it is a controversy; **C6 caps the pool**, and **C2** makes personal recovery actions socially toxic.
*Adapt:* redesign malus/clawback as a **reputational and pensionable-service** consequence rather than a cash one. The cash pool is too small to deter; service credit is not.

### 🟡 RESERVE

**P-10 — Whistleblowing channel with protection and a duty to act** · *Fit 3 · Feasibility 3*
*Origin:* corporate integrity and speak-up programmes.
*Why it is only 3:* it supports Ch. V integrity, but it is **not primarily a performance-consequence mechanism**, and the evidence on effectiveness in small states is weak.
*Adapt:* route it to a **regional or external** body. **C2** is the reason — in a society where everyone knows everyone, a locally administered anonymous channel is anonymous in name only.

## 3. What each constraint actually does to the matrix

Use this table in Chapter 5. It is the mechanism that turns "these practices did not transfer" into "these practices cannot transfer, *for these reasons*."

| Constraint | Which axis it depresses | Practices it most affects | What it forces |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C1** small talent pool | **Feasibility** | P-02, P-07, P-08 | Regional pooling of scarce expertise; fewer, not more, oversight bodies |
| **C2** social/political proximity | **Feasibility** | P-05, P-08, P-10 | Consequences that are applied by **rule and role**, not in person — P-09's logic |
| **C3** diseconomies of scale | **Feasibility** | P-01, P-07, P-10 | Reuse existing bodies and existing reports; never create a standing unit |
| **C4** natural monopolies | **Fit** (measurement validity) | P-01, P-03 | Self-referential metrics instead of comparators — the OECD concedes this problem |
| **C5** exposure to shocks | **Fit** (target credibility) | P-03, P-06 | Built-in variance tolerance and force-majeure clauses, or every missed target gets a weather excuse |
| **C6** fiscal constraint | **Feasibility** | P-05, P-06, P-08 | Terminally softened sanctions; graduated ladders; non-cash consequences |

> **The analytical punchline of your own matrix:** 🟢 Transfer Now contains **procedural** mechanisms (dashboards, evaluations, scorecards, disclosure tables, block-on-send reporting). 🟠 Adapt First contains **substantive** mechanisms (exit regimes, hard budgets, pay consequences). **Procedural mechanisms transfer; substantive ones do not.** That is because procedural mechanisms create consequences out of *information and roles*, whereas substantive ones require an independent actor, a comparator, or a credible threat — precisely the three things C1, C4 and C6 remove.
>
> That single observation is a publishable-quality finding and it comes straight out of your own data. Make it the centrepiece of Chapter 5.

## 4. Where to source further private-sector practice (and how to keep it honest)

| Sector | Why relevant | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Aviation ground handling & airport operations** | Safety-critical, procedurally enforced, high-consequence — the environment your artifact comes from | Safety regimes are backed by regulatory power that SOE governance is not. Explain the disanalogy rather than hiding it |
| **Banking & financial services** | The strongest available real-world evidence on performance consequences, because supervisors *do* use them | Requires a regulator with statutory independence — **C1 and C2 bite hard.** Use as contrast, not aspiration |
| **Telecommunications & utilities (regulated)** | Directly relevant: regulated private monopolies face the same **C4** no-comparator problem | This is your best source of *coping mechanisms* for C4 |
| **Ports & maritime** | Closest structural analogue to Caribbean SOE portfolios — often state-owned elsewhere too | Check whether the comparator is itself an SOE, which weakens the "private-sector transfer" framing |
| **Hospitals & health systems** | Rich literature on performance management where the organisation cannot be allowed to fail — i.e. **C6 already solved** | The most directly transferable literature for the C6 problem |

> **One methodological caution:** the "private sector" is not a single template. Say precisely which private sector, and why it is the right comparator for the practice you are testing. Ground handling, retail banking and telecoms produce very different accountability regimes. **A marker will penalise "the private sector" as an undefined category.**

## 5. Chapter 5 recommendation skeleton

Fill this once your matrix is scored:

> The PCGI analysis identifies a consequence gap concentrated in dimension D5. Transferring private-sector practice to address it is constrained by six structural features of small states. Practices were scored on governance fit and SIDS feasibility, producing a clear separation: **procedural mechanisms achieved both high fit and high feasibility, whereas substantive mechanisms achieved high fit but low feasibility.**
>
> This study accordingly recommends the priority adoption of [P-01, P-02, P-03, P-04, P-09] — mechanisms that generate consequences from information, roles and pre-commitment rather than from independent actors, competitive comparators, or credible threats of failure, all of which the preceding constraint analysis showed to be unavailable. [P-05, P-06, P-07, P-08] are recommended for adaptation rather than direct transfer, in each case softened at the specific point where the relevant constraint bites (see Table X).
>
> Extending this logic, the study recommends that the block-on-send discipline already evidenced at the operational layer [Appendix C] be applied upward to the reporting relationship between SOEs and their ownership entities, so that failure to report becomes structurally visible rather than merely unlawful.
